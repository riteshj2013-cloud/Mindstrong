- **options**:
  - A) √5
  - B) 2 + √5
  - C) 1 + √5
  - D) √6
- **answer**: C
- **explanation**: The rectangle is 2 units wide and 1 unit tall, and its bottom-left corner is at 1. Its diagonal is √(2² + 1²) = √5 long. Choosing √5 is tempting because it is the length of the diagonal, but the arc starts at 1, not at 0.
- **hint**: The arc in Lakshay's figure keeps the diagonal's length; find that length, then notice where on the line the arc is centred.

- **alt**: A right-angled triangle with the right angle at the bottom left. The upright side is labelled (√3 + 1) cm, the base is labelled (√3 − 1) cm and the sloping side is marked ?.
- **options**:
  - A) 2√3 cm
  - B) 2√2 cm
  - C) 2 cm
  - D) 8 cm
- **answer**: B
- **explanation**: (√3 + 1)² = 3 + 2√3 + 1 = 4 + 2√3 and (√3 − 1)² = 4 − 2√3. By Pythagoras, the longest side squared is (4 + 2√3) + (4 − 2√3) = 8, so the longest side is √8 = 2√2 cm (about 2.83 cm). Choosing 2√3 cm is tempting because it adds the two shorter sides, (√3 + 1) + (√3 − 1), but the longest side comes from adding their squares, not the sides themselves.
- **hint**: Square each labelled side of Kalpana's triangle with an identity; the middle terms cancel when you add the results.
- **skill**: surd_sides_of_a_right_triangle

### Q03
- **id**: g9-maths-ch01-ach-x-03
- **stem**: Nirali adds 0.333… (the digit 3 repeating) and 0.4545… (the block 45 repeating) and writes the sum as p/q in lowest terms. What is p + q?
- **options**:
  - A) 59
  - B) 107
  - C) 33
  - D) 92
- **answer**: A
- **explanation**: 0.333… = 3/9 = 1/3, and for 0.4545… a two-digit block repeats, so it equals 45/99 = 5/11. The sum is 1/3 + 5/11 = 11/33 + 15/33 = 26/33, which is in lowest terms, so p + q = 26 + 33 = 59. 107 is tempting because it treats 0.4545… as 45/100, giving 1/3 + 9/20 = 47/60, but a repeating block of two digits is over 99, not 100.
- **hint**: Turn each of Nirali's repeating decimals into a fraction using nines, add, and cancel before reading off p and q.
- **skill**: adding_recurring_decimals_as_fractions
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers

### Q04
- **id**: g9-maths-ch01-ach-x-04
- **stem**: For a maths-club challenge, Darshit writes x = 1/(3 − 2√2) on a card and asks his friends to work out x² + 1/x² without a calculator. What value should they get?
- **options**:
  - A) 36
  - B) 17
  - C) 32
  - D) 34
- **answer**: D
- **explanation**: Multiply the top and bottom of x by (3 + 2√2): x = (3 + 2√2)/(9 − 8) = 3 + 2√2. Then 1/x = 3 − 2√2, so x + 1/x = 6. Squaring, x² + 2 + 1/x² = 36, so x² + 1/x² = 34. 36 is tempting because it is (x + 1/x)², which still holds the extra 2 from the middle term 2 × x × 1/x.
- **hint**: Rationalise Darshit's x first; x and 1/x then form a neat pair whose sum is easy, and squaring that sum helps.
- **skill**: rationalising_then_using_x_plus_reciprocal
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers

### Q05
- **id**: g9-maths-ch01-ach-x-05
- **stem**: At a textile shop in Surat, Parineeti has three ribbons of lengths (√7 + √5) m, (√8 + 2) m and 2√6 m. Which ribbon is the longest?
- **options**:
  - A) (√8 + 2) m
  - B) (√7 + √5) m
  - C) 2√6 m
  - D) All three are equal
- **answer**: C
- **explanation**: Compare the squares. (√7 + √5)² = 12 + 2√35, (√8 + 2)² = 8 + 4√8 + 4 = 12 + 2√32, and (2√6)² = 24 = 12 + 2√36. All three start with 12, and √36 is the biggest of √35, √32 and √36, so the 2√6 m ribbon is the longest. Choosing (√8 + 2) m is tempting because √8 is the biggest single root on show, but once each length is squared its extra part is only 2√32.
- **hint**: Square each of Parineeti's ribbon lengths; all three squares begin with the same whole number, so compare what is left.
- **skill**: comparing_sums_of_surds_by_squaring
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers
