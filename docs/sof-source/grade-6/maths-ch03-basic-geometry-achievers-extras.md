- **alt**: Triangle ABC stands on base BC, and the base is carried on in a straight line past C to D. The angle at A is marked 48°, the outside angle ACD between CA and CD is marked 115°, and the angle at B is marked with a question mark.
- **options**:
  - A) 67°
  - B) 17°
  - C) 65°
  - D) 57°
- **answer**: A
- **explanation**: B, C and D lie on one straight line, so ∠ACB = 180° − 115° = 65°. The angles of triangle ABC add up to 180°, so ∠ABC = 180° − 48° − 65° = 67°. As a check, the outside angle 115° equals the two far inside angles together: 48° + 67° = 115°. 17° is tempting because it puts 115° into the angle sum as if it were inside the triangle, but 115° is the outside angle at C.
- **hint**: The two angles at C along Harshil's straight line BCD make a straight angle together; find the inside angle at C first.

### Q02
- **id**: g6-maths-ch03-ach-x-02
- **stem**: Hetvi chooses three angles. ∠P and ∠Q are supplementary, ∠Q and ∠R are complementary, and ∠P is 4 times ∠R. What is ∠Q?
- **options**:
  - A) 30°
  - B) 120°
  - C) 36°
  - D) 60°
- **answer**: D
- **explanation**: ∠P + ∠Q = 180° and ∠Q + ∠R = 90°, so ∠P is 90° more than ∠R. If ∠R is one part, ∠P is 4 parts, so the 3 extra parts make 90° and one part is 30°. So ∠R = 30°, ∠P = 120° and ∠Q = 90° − 30° = 60°. 30° is tempting because it is ∠R, the first angle found, but the question asks for ∠Q.
- **hint**: Both facts about ∠Q show how much bigger ∠P is than ∠R; then split that gap using ∠P = 4 × ∠R.
- **skill**: supplementary_complementary_chain
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers

### Q04
- **id**: g6-maths-ch03-ach-x-04
- **stem**: On her rangoli board, Neelima draws a straight line AOB and three rays OC, OD and OE on the same side of it, in that order from OA. ∠AOC = 30°, ∠COD = 45°, ∠DOE = 50° and ∠EOB = 55°. Taking the five rays two at a time, how many of the angles formed are obtuse?
- **options**:
  - A) 5
  - B) 0
  - C) 4
  - D) 3
- **answer**: C
- **explanation**: Five rays taken two at a time make 10 angles. Adding neighbouring angles gives ∠AOD = 75°, ∠AOE = 125°, ∠COE = 95°, ∠COB = 150° and ∠DOB = 105°; there are also the four marked angles (30°, 45°, 50° and 55°) and the straight angle AOB = 180°. The obtuse ones, more than 90° but less than 180°, are 125°, 95°, 150° and 105°, so there are 4. 5 is tempting because it also counts ∠AOB, but 180° is a straight angle, not an obtuse angle.
- **hint**: Add neighbouring marked angles on Neelima's line to get the bigger angles, then check each against the limits for an obtuse angle.
- **skill**: count_obtuse_angles_on_a_straight_line
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers

### Q05
- **id**: g6-maths-ch03-ach-x-05
- **stem**: At the school science mela, Lakshit's robot toy faces north. It turns 135° clockwise and then 260° anticlockwise. What is the smaller angle between the direction it faced at the start and the direction it faces now?
- **options**:
  - A) 125°
  - B) 35°
  - C) 235°
  - D) 55°
- **answer**: A
- **explanation**: Turning 135° clockwise and then 260° anticlockwise leaves the robot 260° − 135° = 125° anticlockwise from north. 125° is less than 180°, so it is the smaller angle; the reflex angle the other way round is 360° − 125° = 235°. 35° is tempting because it adds the two turns, 135° + 260° = 395°, and takes away a full turn, but the turns go in opposite directions, so one must be taken away from the other.
- **hint**: Lakshit's robot turns in opposite directions, so the second turn partly undoes the earlier one; work out where it ends up compared with north.
- **skill**: net_turn_smaller_angle
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers
