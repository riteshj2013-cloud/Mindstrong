## Interactive lesson outline
- L1: Welcome. Today we practise spoken English the way it appears in dialogues, debates and real conversations.
- L2: Good speaking is not only correct grammar. It is knowing when to take a turn, how to disagree politely, and how to keep a point clear.
- L3: Discourse markers are the road signs of talk: "firstly," "on the other hand," "to sum up." They guide the listener.
- L4: In a debate, the proposition side argues for the motion; the opposition argues against it. Each side needs claims, reasons and evidence.
- L5: A rebuttal answers the other side's strongest point — it does not merely repeat your own speech.
- L6: (optional) Softeners such as "I see your point, but…" or "Would you mind if…" keep disagreement respectful.
- L7: Casual chat allows contractions and fillers ("um," "you know"). A school debate needs clearer, more formal spoken English.
- L8: Let's try a tiny example. Motion: "School uniforms should be optional."
- L9: Weak opening: "Uniforms are boring and I hate them." Stronger: "Uniforms limit self-expression without improving learning."
- L10: The second version states a claim listeners can test. That is debate language.
- L11: In dialogue, listen for purpose: Is the speaker requesting, refusing, persuading or clarifying?
- L12: (optional) When two replies seem polite, choose the one that matches the situation's formality.
- L13: Now it's your turn. Read each dialogue or speech carefully and ask what the speaker is doing with language.
- L14: Well done. Clear speech wins arguments — and friends. Let's begin.

## Passage bank (for quizzes)

### D1 — Dialogue: Before the Inter-House Debate
**Anya:** Have you finished your opening speech for tomorrow?
**Rohan:** Almost. The motion is "Social media does more harm than good for teenagers." I'm on the proposition.
**Anya:** So you argue that it harms more than it helps. What's your strongest point?
**Rohan:** Firstly, endless scrolling steals study time. Secondly, comparison culture damages self-esteem. Finally, cyberbullying is hard to escape.
**Anya:** That's clear. I'm opposition. On the other hand, social media helps students organise campaigns and find reliable study groups.
**Rohan:** Fair enough. May I suggest you prepare a rebuttal for my "study time" point? Judges notice when you answer the other side.
**Anya:** Would you mind if we practise for ten minutes after lunch?
**Rohan:** Not at all. Let's get cracking — and please don't use "like" every second word in the hall.
**Anya:** Point taken. Formal register it is.

### P1 — Opening speech (proposition)
Honourable judges, teachers and friends: the motion before us today is that homework should be limited to one hour on school nights. We on the proposition firmly support this motion. Firstly, research shows that after about sixty minutes of focused work, concentration falls sharply for most adolescents. Secondly, students need time for sport, family meals and rest — none of which are luxuries. Critics may claim that less homework means less learning, but we argue that shorter, well-designed tasks teach more than long, rushed ones. To sum up, limiting homework protects health without lowering standards. I urge you to vote for the motion.

### P2 — Opposition rebuttal
Thank you. The proposition painted a neat picture, but it skips an important fact. Many subjects — mathematics and languages especially — need spaced practice beyond a single hour. If we cap homework rigidly, weaker students lose the extra support they need at home. Moreover, "one hour" is not equal for everyone: a quiet desk at home is not the same as a crowded shared room. We on the opposition therefore reject a blanket limit. Instead, we propose clearer guidelines and better-designed tasks, not a one-size-fits-all clock. I ask the house to oppose the motion.
