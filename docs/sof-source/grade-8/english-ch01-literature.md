## Interactive lesson outline
- L1: Welcome back. Today we're going to read the way critics and writers read: not just for what happens, but for why it matters.
- L2: Every strong piece of writing has layers. On top is the plot. Underneath are theme, tone and purpose.
- L3: Theme is the big idea the writer wants you to carry away. It's rarely stated outright, so you have to build it from evidence.
- L4: Tone is the writer's attitude. Is the voice hopeful, bitter, playful, quietly sad? Word choice is your strongest clue.
- L5: Purpose asks why the text exists. To inform, to persuade, to entertain, or to make you feel something?
- L6: Then there's figurative language: similes, metaphors, personification. These aren't decoration. Each one is a deliberate choice that shapes meaning.
- L7: (optional) A quick test for figurative language: if a sentence can't be literally true, ask what it is really saying.
- L8: Let's try a short example together. "The old school bus coughed its way up the hill, complaining at every bend. Inside, forty children sang louder, as if their voices could push it to the top."
- L9: First, the figurative language. A bus can't cough or complain, so that's personification. It makes the bus feel tired and ancient.
- L10: Now inference. Why do the children sing louder? They aren't worried. They're turning a struggle into a game.
- L11: So the tone is affectionate and cheerful, and a possible theme is how shared joy can carry us through difficulty.
- L12: Notice that every claim we made came from a specific word or phrase. That's the habit of a careful reader.
- L13: (optional) When two answers both seem right, choose the one the text supports most fully, not the one that's merely possible.
- L14: Now it's over to you. Read each passage slowly, underline the words that carry feeling, and ask what the writer is doing with them.
- L15: (optional) For poems, read them aloud in your head. Rhythm and sound are clues as well.
- L16: Excellent. Reading between the lines is a skill you'll use in every subject and well beyond school. Let's begin.

## Passage bank (for quizzes)

### P1 — The Last Repair Shop on Kothi Road (prose fiction)
For forty-one years, the sign above Hamid Chacha's shop had promised the same thing: WE FIX WHAT OTHERS THROW AWAY. The paint had faded to the colour of weak tea, but the promise had not. Inside, among coils of copper wire and the sweet, burnt smell of solder, transistor radios sat in patient rows like old patients in a waiting room.

Tanvi first came in with a phone whose screen had shattered into a spider's web. The showroom in the mall had told her it was "not worth repairing" and suggested a newer model. Hamid Chacha turned the phone over in his rough hands, peering at it through glasses as thick as bottle bottoms. "Not worth repairing," he repeated softly, as if tasting a strange fruit. "Everything is worth repairing. The question is whether we are worth the patience."

He needed three days. Tanvi visited every afternoon, at first to check on her phone and then, without quite admitting it, to watch him work. She learned that a resistor has coloured stripes that tell its story, and that most broken things are only one small fault away from working again.

When she finally collected the phone, it was not new. There was a hairline scratch near the camera. But it was hers, and it worked, and Tanvi found she liked it better that way.

### P2 — Where Have All the Stars Gone? (non-fiction essay)
Ask your grandparents what the night sky looked like when they were children, and many will describe something you may never have seen: a sky so crowded with stars that the Milky Way stretched across it like a spilled river of salt. Today, in most Indian cities, a child can count the visible stars on two hands.

The culprit is not cloud or dust alone. It is light, our own light, pouring upward from streetlamps, hoardings and buildings that glow all night whether anyone needs them or not. Scientists call this light pollution, and its effects reach far beyond astronomy. Migrating birds, which navigate partly by the stars, become confused and circle lit towers until they are exhausted. Sea turtle hatchlings on the beaches of Odisha crawl towards bright roads instead of the moonlit sea. Even our own sleep suffers when our bodies cannot tell night from day.

The encouraging news is that, unlike many forms of pollution, this one can be reversed almost instantly. Shielded lamps that point downward, timers on advertising boards, and simply switching off what we are not using could return the stars to our cities. The darkness, it turns out, is worth protecting.

### P3 — The Banyan at the Crossroads (original poem)
I was here before the tarmac, before the signal's blinking eye,
when bullock carts were traffic and the kites owned all the sky.
I have sheltered tired vendors, I have heard the elders' words,
I have been a home to monkeys and a parliament of birds.

They hung their wishes on my branches, threads of red and gold;
they told me secrets in the summer that they never should have told.
Now the city hums around me like a hive that never sleeps,
and the road grows ever wider, and the concrete climbs and creeps.

They say I am an obstacle; they measure me in feet.
They have drawn a dotted line through me upon a planning sheet.
But my roots run under every house, beneath each hurried stride —
cut me down, and you will find the town was standing on my side.
