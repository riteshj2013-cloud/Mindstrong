# Chapter 3: Metals and Non-Metals

## Meta
- Grade: 8
- Subject: Science
- Theme tags: metals, non-metals, physical properties, chemical properties, reactivity, rusting, displacement, uses
- Source basis: NCERT Class 8 / typical NSO themes (original items only)

## Learning objectives
- Compare metals and non-metals by their physical properties: lustre, hardness, malleability, ductility, sonority, and conduction of heat and electricity.
- Recognise important exceptions, such as soft sodium, liquid mercury, liquid bromine, lustrous iodine, and the two forms of carbon (diamond and graphite).
- Describe how metals and non-metals react with oxygen, water, acids, and bases, and classify metal oxides as basic and non-metal oxides as acidic.
- Use a simple reactivity series to predict displacement reactions, such as iron or zinc placed in copper sulphate solution.
- Explain the conditions needed for rusting of iron and the common methods used to prevent it.
- Link the properties of metals and non-metals to their uses in daily life.

## Interactive lesson outline

### Scene 1: Feel the difference — physical properties
- **Visual:** A lab bench with samples: an iron nail, copper wire, aluminium foil, a lump of sulphur, a piece of coal, and a pencil lead. A virtual hammer, a wire-drawing machine, a bell striker, and a battery-bulb circuit sit beside them.
- **TTS:**
  - "Metals are usually shiny. This shine is called lustre."
  - "Metals can be beaten into thin sheets. This is malleability."
  - "Metals can be drawn into thin wires. This is ductility."
  - "Metals make a ringing sound when struck. This is sonority."
  - "Metals are good conductors of heat and electricity."
  - "Non-metals like sulphur and coal are dull and brittle. They break into pieces when hammered."
- **Interaction:** Test each sample with each tool. The hammer flattens the metals and shatters sulphur. The circuit bulb glows for the metals. Results fill a two-column table: Metal or Non-metal.

### Scene 2: The rule breakers — exceptions
- **Visual:** A "Surprise Shelf" with cards: sodium being cut by a knife, a bead of mercury rolling in a dish, a bottle of reddish-brown bromine, shiny purple-black iodine crystals, a sparkling diamond, and a grey graphite pencil lead lighting a bulb.
- **TTS:**
  - "Not every element follows the rules."
  - "Sodium and potassium are metals, but they are so soft you can cut them with a knife."
  - "Mercury is a metal, yet it is a liquid at room temperature."
  - "Bromine is a non-metal that is also a liquid."
  - "Iodine is a non-metal, but it has lustre."
  - "Diamond and graphite are both forms of carbon, a non-metal."
  - "Diamond is extremely hard and does not conduct electricity. Graphite is soft and slippery, and it does conduct electricity."
- **Interaction:** Odd-one-out cards. The learner taps the property that breaks the usual rule for each element. Feedback names the exception.

### Scene 3: Reactions with oxygen, water, acids, and bases
- **Visual:** Four stations. Station 1: magnesium ribbon burns with a dazzling white flame; its ash is dissolved in water and tested with litmus. Station 2: sulphur burns in a jar; the gas is dissolved in water and tested with litmus. Station 3: a small piece of sodium fizzes and darts on water. Station 4: zinc granules bubble in dilute hydrochloric acid, and a burning splint near the gas gives a pop.
- **TTS:**
  - "Metals burn in oxygen to form metal oxides. These oxides are basic. Their solutions turn red litmus blue."
  - "Non-metals like sulphur form oxides that are acidic. Their solutions turn blue litmus red."
  - "Sodium reacts so strongly with water that it is stored in kerosene."
  - "White phosphorus catches fire in air, so it is stored in water."
  - "Many metals react with dilute acids to give hydrogen gas. Hydrogen burns with a pop sound."
  - "Non-metals generally do not react with dilute acids."
  - "Some metals, like aluminium, also react with sodium hydroxide to give hydrogen."
- **Interaction:** Predict-then-test. Before each reaction, the learner chooses the litmus colour or the gas formed. A wrong guess replays the reaction slowly with a hint.

### Scene 4: Who is stronger? — Reactivity and displacement
- **Visual:** A ladder labelled with metals from top to bottom: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, lead, (hydrogen), copper, mercury, silver, gold. Beside it, three beakers: an iron nail in blue copper sulphate, a zinc strip in copper sulphate, and a copper strip in green iron sulphate.
- **TTS:**
  - "Metals higher on the reactivity ladder are more reactive."
  - "A more reactive metal can push a less reactive metal out of its salt solution. This is a displacement reaction."
  - "Iron pushes copper out of copper sulphate. The blue solution turns light green, and a reddish-brown coating forms on the nail."
  - "Zinc does the same. The blue solution becomes colourless."
  - "Copper is less reactive than iron, so nothing happens when copper is placed in iron sulphate."
- **Interaction:** Drag a metal strip into a salt solution. The learner first predicts "Reaction" or "No reaction" using the ladder. The beaker then shows the colour change or stays the same.

### Scene 5: Rusting and everyday uses
- **Visual:** Three test tubes with iron nails: Tube A in dry air with calcium chloride, Tube B in boiled water with an oil layer on top, Tube C in ordinary tap water open to air. After a week, only Tube C shows rust. Then a city street appears with a painted gate, a galvanised bucket, copper wires, aluminium foil, a gold ring, a chlorine tablet in a water tank, and a fertiliser bag.
- **TTS:**
  - "Iron rusts only when both oxygen and water are present."
  - "Rust is a reddish-brown flaky substance, mainly hydrated iron oxide."
  - "Salty, moist air near the sea makes iron rust faster."
  - "We prevent rusting by painting, oiling, greasing, or galvanising. Galvanising means coating iron with zinc."
  - "Copper and aluminium make electric wires. Aluminium foil wraps food. Gold and silver make jewellery."
  - "Non-metals are useful too. We breathe oxygen. Nitrogen and phosphorus are used in fertilisers. Chlorine purifies water. Iodine solution is an antiseptic."
- **Interaction:** First, the learner taps the tube that will rust and explains why using a choice card. Then a matching game links each object on the street to the property or element behind it.

### Q2
**Stem:** Which metal is a liquid at room temperature?
**Options:**
A) Sodium
B) Aluminium
C) Bromine
D) Mercury
**Answer:** D
**Explanation:** Mercury is the metal that is liquid at room temperature. Bromine is also a liquid, but it is a non-metal.

### Q3
**Stem:** Which of these non-metallic substances is a good conductor of electricity?
**Options:**
A) Graphite
B) Sulphur
C) Diamond
D) Iodine
**Answer:** A
**Explanation:** Graphite is a form of carbon that conducts electricity, which is why it is used in electrodes. Diamond, also carbon, does not conduct.

### Q4
**Stem:** A temple bell gives a ringing sound when struck. This property of metals is called —
**Options:**
A) Malleability
B) Ductility
C) Sonority
D) Brittleness
**Answer:** C
**Explanation:** Sonority is the property of producing a ringing sound when struck. Most metals are sonorous.

### Q5
**Stem:** Which metal is kept under kerosene in the laboratory?
**Options:**
A) Copper
B) Iron
C) Sodium
D) Zinc
**Answer:** C
**Explanation:** Sodium reacts strongly with oxygen and with moisture in the air. Kerosene keeps air and water away from it.

### Q6
**Stem:** Electric wires are commonly made of copper because copper is —
**Options:**
A) A good conductor of electricity and ductile
B) Brittle and lustrous
C) A poor conductor of heat
D) Soft enough to cut with a knife
**Answer:** A
**Explanation:** Copper conducts electricity very well and can be drawn into thin wires. Both properties make it ideal for wiring.

**Stem:** The picture shows an iron nail in copper sulphate solution at the start (tube 1) and the same tube after 30 minutes (tube 2). What colour change is seen in the solution?
**Options:**
A) Colourless to blue
B) Blue to light green
C) Light green to blue
D) No change at all
**Answer:** B
**Explanation:** Iron is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from the solution. Iron sulphate forms, which is light green, and brown copper coats the nail. Light green to blue is the reverse: the blue copper sulphate is used up, not made.
### Q12

**Options:**
A) Sulphur
B) Carbon
C) Iodine
D) White phosphorus
**Answer:** D
**Explanation:** White phosphorus is very reactive and burns in air. Storing it under water keeps air away.

### Q13
**Stem:** Sulphur is burnt and the gas formed is dissolved in water. The solution will —
**Options:**
A) Turn red litmus blue
B) Be basic in nature
C) Be neutral to litmus
D) Turn blue litmus red
**Answer:** D
**Explanation:** Burning sulphur forms sulphur dioxide, an acidic oxide. In water it forms sulphurous acid, which turns blue litmus red.

**Explanation:** Iodine is a non-metal, yet its crystals are shiny. It is an exception to the rule that non-metals are dull.

### Q16
**Stem:** Which metal is soft enough to be cut with a knife?
**Options:**
A) Sodium
B) Iron
C) Copper
