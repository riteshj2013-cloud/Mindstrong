# Chapter 1: Cell — Structure and Functions

## Meta
- Grade: 8
- Subject: Science
- Theme tags: cell, organelles, plant cell, animal cell, microscope, unicellular, multicellular
- Source basis: NCERT Class 8 / typical NSO themes (original items only)

## Learning objectives
- Describe how cells were discovered (Hooke, Leeuwenhoek, Brown) and state the cell theory in simple words.
- Name the main parts of a compound microscope, work out total magnification, and describe how to prepare and stain a simple slide.
- Identify the cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, vacuoles, chloroplasts, and cell wall, and state what each one does.
- Compare plant and animal cells, and use clues about their structures to work out where a cell came from.
- Tell unicellular organisms from multicellular ones, and connect cell shape to cell function.
- Arrange the levels of organisation in order: cell, tissue, organ, organ system.

## Interactive lesson outline

### Scene 1: Tiny rooms in a cork slice
- **Visual:** A 1665-style workshop. Robert Hooke slices cork thinly and looks through his microscope. The view zooms into a honeycomb of empty boxes labelled "cells". A timeline slides in: Hooke (1665, cork cells), Leeuwenhoek (1670s, living cells in pond water), Brown (1831, nucleus), cell theory (1830s to 1850s).
- **TTS:**
  - "In 1665, Robert Hooke looked at a thin slice of cork and saw tiny boxes. He called them cells."
  - "Cork cells are dead, so Hooke saw only their empty walls."
  - "Leeuwenhoek made his own lenses and was the first to see living cells moving in pond water."
  - "In 1831, Robert Brown discovered a round body inside cells, called the nucleus."
  - "Cell theory says that all living things are made of cells, and new cells come from existing cells."
- **Interaction:** Timeline drag-and-drop. Match each scientist card to his discovery. A correct match shows a short fact; a wrong one springs back with a hint.

### Scene 2: Meet the microscope
- **Visual:** A labelled 3D compound microscope that can be rotated: eyepiece, body tube, objective lenses, stage, stage clips, mirror or light, coarse and fine adjustment knobs. Beside it, a slide-making strip shows how to peel onion skin, add a drop of safranin and a drop of glycerine, and lower the cover slip at an angle.
- **TTS:**
  - "Most cells are too small to see with our eyes alone. We measure them in micrometres."
  - "A compound microscope uses two lenses: the eyepiece and the objective."
  - "To find total magnification, multiply the two. A 10x eyepiece with a 40x objective gives 400x."
  - "Use the coarse knob to focus roughly, then the fine knob to make the image sharp."
  - "Stains such as safranin colour cell parts, and glycerine stops the peel from drying out."
- **Interaction:** Tap each part to hear its name and job. Then a magnification mini-game: pick an eyepiece and an objective, type the total magnification, and see the onion cells zoom in to match.

### Scene 3: Inside the cell city
- **Visual:** A cell drawn as a walled city. The cell membrane is the gate, the cytoplasm is the busy streets, the nucleus is the control room holding chromosomes, mitochondria are power stations, vacuoles are storage tanks, chloroplasts are solar panels, and the cell wall is the outer fortress wall. Each part glows when tapped.
- **TTS:**
  - "The cell membrane is selectively permeable. It lets some substances in and out and blocks others."
  - "Cytoplasm is the jelly-like material where the organelles float and many reactions take place."
  - "The nucleus controls the cell. Its chromosomes carry genes passed from parents to offspring."
  - "Mitochondria are the powerhouses. They release energy from food."
  - "Chloroplasts contain green chlorophyll and make food by photosynthesis."
  - "Plant cells also have a strong cell wall made of cellulose, and a large central vacuole."
- **Interaction:** Function match. Function cards ("releases energy", "controls entry and exit", "makes food", "stores cell sap", "gives support", "controls the cell") are dragged onto the correct organelle. A wrong drop shows the city analogy as a hint.

### Scene 4: Plant cell versus animal cell
- **Visual:** Split screen. On the left, a rectangular onion or leaf cell with a cell wall, large central vacuole, and chloroplasts in the leaf version. On the right, an irregular cheek cell with only a membrane and small vacuoles. The parts both cells share light up in yellow; the parts only plant cells have light up in green.
- **TTS:**
  - "Plant and animal cells both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus, and mitochondria."
  - "Only plant cells have a cell wall and chloroplasts."
  - "Plant cells usually have one large central vacuole. Animal cells have small vacuoles, or none at all."
  - "Onion peel cells are plant cells without chloroplasts, because they grow away from light."
  - "Because there is no wall, animal cells have irregular shapes."
- **Interaction:** Cell detective. A mystery cell appears with three clues, for example "wall: yes, chloroplasts: no, large vacuole: yes". The child picks its source: leaf, onion peel, cheek, or blood. Feedback explains which clue decided the answer.

### Scene 5: One cell or many, and how they team up
- **Visual:** On the left, a drop of pond water with an Amoeba (pseudopodia moving), a Paramecium, and bacteria (with "no nuclear membrane" tags). On the right, a zoom-out from a single muscle cell to muscle tissue, to the stomach, to the digestive system, and finally to the whole human. A gallery shows cell shapes: long, branched nerve cell; biconcave red blood cell; spindle-shaped muscle cell.
- **TTS:**
  - "Unicellular organisms, like Amoeba and bacteria, do everything with just one cell."
  - "Bacteria have no nuclear membrane. Scientists call cells like these prokaryotic."
  - "Multicellular organisms divide up the work among many cells."
  - "A cell's shape suits its job. Long nerve cells carry messages, and disc-shaped red blood cells carry oxygen."
  - "Similar cells form tissues, tissues form organs, and organs form organ systems."
- **Interaction:** Two quick tasks. First, sort organisms into Unicellular or Multicellular bins. Second, arrange cell, tissue, organ, and organ system cards from smallest to largest. A recap card then lists the key ideas: discovery, microscope, organelles, plant versus animal cells, and levels of organisation.

## Quiz Set A

### Q1
**Stem:** Who first used the word "cell" to describe the tiny box-like spaces he saw under a microscope?
**Options:**
A) Anton van Leeuwenhoek
B) Robert Hooke
C) Robert Brown
D) Matthias Schleiden
**Answer:** B
**Explanation:** Robert Hooke used the word "cell" in 1665 after looking at a thin slice of cork. The spaces reminded him of small rooms. Leeuwenhoek was the first to see living cells, and Robert Brown discovered the nucleus.

### Q2
**Stem:** When Robert Hooke looked at his first cells in 1665, what material was he observing?
**Options:**
A) Onion peel
B) Human cheek lining
C) A green leaf
D) A thin slice of cork
**Answer:** D
**Explanation:** Hooke looked at cork, which comes from tree bark. Cork cells are dead, so he saw only their empty walls arranged like a honeycomb, not any living contents.

### Q3
**Stem:** What is called the basic structural and functional unit of all living things?
**Options:**
A) Cell
B) Tissue
C) Organ
D) Nucleus
**Answer:** A
**Explanation:** Every living thing is made of one or more cells, and every life activity happens inside cells. Tissues and organs are made of cells, and the nucleus is only one part of a cell.

### Q4
**Stem:** Which part of a compound microscope keeps the glass slide firmly in place on the stage?
**Options:**
A) Eyepiece
B) Objective lens
C) Stage clips
D) Coarse adjustment knob
**Answer:** C
**Explanation:** Stage clips press the slide onto the stage so it does not slide around. The eyepiece and objective lens magnify the image, and the coarse adjustment knob is used for focusing.

### Q5
**Stem:** The cell membrane is called selectively permeable. What does this mean?
**Options:**
A) It lets every substance pass freely
B) It stops every substance from passing
C) It lets some substances pass in and out but blocks others
D) It is made of tough cellulose
**Answer:** C
**Explanation:** "Selectively permeable" means the membrane chooses what passes. Useful things such as water and oxygen can enter, and wastes can leave, while many other substances are kept out. Cellulose makes up the plant cell wall, not the membrane.

**Options:**
A) Part P
B) Part Q
C) Part R
D) Part S
**Answer:** A
**Explanation:** Part P is a mitochondrion, the powerhouse of the cell: it breaks down food such as glucose using oxygen and releases energy. Part Q, the large round nucleus, controls the cell's work but does not release energy from food.
### Q7
**Stem:** Which of these structures is found in plant cells but NOT in animal cells?
**Options:**
A) Cell membrane
B) Nucleus
C) Mitochondria
D) Cell wall
**Answer:** D
**Explanation:** Plant and animal cells both have a cell membrane, a nucleus, and mitochondria. Only plant cells have a cell wall, a stiff outer layer that sits outside the cell membrane.

### Q8
**Stem:** What is the plant cell wall mainly made of?
**Options:**
A) Protein
B) Cellulose
C) Starch
D) Chitin
**Answer:** B
**Explanation:** The plant cell wall is made mostly of cellulose, a strong carbohydrate made of long fibres. Starch is stored food, not a wall material. Chitin is found in the cell walls of fungi and in insect shells.

### Q9
**Stem:** Which green pigment inside chloroplasts traps sunlight for photosynthesis?
**Options:**
A) Chlorophyll
B) Haemoglobin
C) Melanin
D) Carotene
**Answer:** A
**Explanation:** Chlorophyll is the green pigment that absorbs light energy so the plant can make food. Haemoglobin carries oxygen in our blood, and melanin colours our skin. Carotene is yellow-orange, not green.

### Q10
**Stem:** What is the jelly-like substance between the cell membrane and the nucleus called?
**Options:**
A) Nucleoplasm
B) Protoplasm
C) Cytoplasm
D) Vacuole
**Answer:** C
**Explanation:** Cytoplasm is the jelly-like material that holds the organelles. Nucleoplasm is the fluid inside the nucleus. Protoplasm means all the living contents of the cell, which is the cytoplasm and the nucleus together.

### Q11
**Stem:** Which of the following organisms is made of just one cell?
**Options:**
A) Earthworm
B) Amoeba
C) Mushroom
D) Hydra
**Answer:** B
**Explanation:** Amoeba is unicellular: one cell carries out feeding, breathing, moving, and reproducing. Earthworms, mushrooms, and Hydra are all multicellular, even though Hydra and mushrooms look simple.

### Q12
**Stem:** Where are genes, which carry characteristics from parents to offspring, found in a cell?
**Options:**
A) In the cell wall
B) In the vacuole
C) In the cytoplasm only
D) On the chromosomes in the nucleus
**Answer:** D
**Explanation:** Genes are found on thread-like chromosomes inside the nucleus. This is why the nucleus controls the cell's activities and passes on characteristics from one generation to the next.

### Q14
**Stem:** A student stains human cheek cells with methylene blue and looks at them under a microscope. Which structure will she NOT see?
**Options:**
A) Nucleus
B) Cell wall
C) Cell membrane
D) Cytoplasm
**Answer:** B
**Explanation:** Cheek cells are animal cells, so they have no cell wall. She will see a thin cell membrane, cytoplasm, and a darkly stained nucleus. Because there is no wall, cheek cells look irregular and flat.

### Q17
**Stem:** Cells from the root of a carrot or a potato tuber usually do NOT have which structure?
**Options:**
A) Mitochondria
B) Chloroplasts
C) Nucleus
D) Cell wall
**Answer:** B
**Explanation:** Roots and tubers grow underground, where there is no light, so their cells do not need chloroplasts. They still have a nucleus, mitochondria, and a cell wall, and they often store food such as starch.
