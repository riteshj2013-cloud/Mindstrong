- **options**:
  - A) 25
  - B) 8
  - C) 15
  - D) 23

### Q02
- **id**: g8-maths-ch02-ach-x-02
- **stem**: Mayank spends 1/3 of his savings on a cricket bat. He then spends ₹120 more than 1/4 of his savings on a pair of shoes. He has ₹380 left. How much did he have in savings at first?
- **options**:
  - A) ₹1,200
  - B) ₹912
  - C) ₹1,500
  - D) ₹960
- **answer**: A
- **explanation**: Let the savings be ₹s. The bat costs s/3 and the shoes cost s/4 + 120, so s − s/3 − (s/4 + 120) = 380. That gives 5s/12 − 120 = 380, so 5s/12 = 500 and s = ₹1,200. Check: bat ₹400, shoes ₹300 + ₹120 = ₹420, and 1,200 − 400 − 420 = 380. ₹912 is tempting because it solves 5s/12 = 380 and forgets the extra ₹120 paid for the shoes.
- **hint**: Write the cost of Mayank's bat and of his shoes in terms of his savings, then subtract both from the savings.
- **skill**: savings_equation_with_fraction_parts
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers

### Q04
- **id**: g8-maths-ch02-ach-x-04
- **stem**: Hrishita's uncle runs a tea shop in Darjeeling. He mixes tea leaves costing ₹400 per kg with tea leaves costing ₹250 per kg to make 15 kg of a blend worth ₹300 per kg. How many kilograms of the ₹400 tea are in the blend?
- **options**:
  - A) 10 kg
  - B) 5 kg
  - C) 7.5 kg
  - D) 6 kg
- **answer**: B
- **explanation**: Let the blend hold k kg of ₹400 tea and (15 − k) kg of ₹250 tea. Its value is 400k + 250(15 − k) = 15 × 300 = 4,500. So 150k + 3,750 = 4,500, 150k = 750 and k = 5 kg. 10 kg is tempting because it is the amount of the ₹250 tea in the blend, not the ₹400 tea; a blend price of ₹300 is closer to ₹250, so the cheaper tea is the larger part.
- **hint**: Let the dearer tea in Hrishita's uncle's blend be some kilograms, write the cheaper tea's share, and match the blend's total value.
- **skill**: mixture_equation_two_prices
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers

### Q05
- **id**: g8-maths-ch02-ach-x-05
- **stem**: In Tanvika's school hall, if 3 children sit on each bench, 8 children have no seat. If 4 children sit on each bench instead, 3 benches are left empty. How many children are there?
- **options**:
  - A) 20
  - B) 60
  - C) 68
  - D) 72
- **answer**: C
- **explanation**: Let there be b benches. The first seating holds 3b children with 8 left over, so there are 3b + 8 children. The second fills b − 3 benches with 4 each, so there are 4(b − 3) children. Then 3b + 8 = 4b − 12, so b = 20 and there are 3 × 20 + 8 = 68 children (check: 4 × 17 = 68). 20 is tempting because it is the number of benches, the unknown in the equation, but the question asks for the number of children.
- **hint**: Let the number of benches in Tanvika's hall be unknown and write the number of children in two ways, one for each seating.
- **skill**: seating_equation_two_arrangements
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers
