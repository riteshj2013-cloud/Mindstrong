## Interactive lesson outline
- L1: Welcome. Today is about precision: choosing exactly the right word, and presenting your writing in exactly the right form.
- L2: English is full of near-twins. Affect and effect. Principal and principle. Stationary and stationery. One letter can change everything.
- L3: Idioms and phrasal verbs say more than their individual words. "Call off" doesn't mean shouting; it means cancelling.
- L4: (optional) A useful trick: if a phrase makes no literal sense in its sentence, it's probably an idiom. Let the context decode it.
- L5: One-word substitutes make writing sharper. Why write "a person who cannot be reformed" when "incorrigible" does the job?
- L6: Now, formats. A notice, an email, a formal letter and a report each follow a pattern that readers expect.
- L7: A notice is brief and factual. It has the issuing body, the date, a clear heading, the key details, and a name and designation at the end.
- L8: A formal letter or email needs a precise subject line, a polite but direct purpose, supporting facts, a clear request, and the right closing.
- L9: Let's test that with a quick example. Imagine a student writes: "Hey, the school bus is always late, fix it!"
- L10: The message is clear, but the tone is wrong for a principal. It sounds like a command to a friend.
- L11: Better: "I wish to bring to your notice that the school bus on Route 4 has been arriving late." Same fact, but respectful and specific.
- L12: That's register: matching your tone to your reader and your purpose.
- L13: (optional) Remember: in a formal letter, "Dear Sir or Madam" ends with "Yours faithfully." A named recipient ends with "Yours sincerely."
- L14: Now it's your turn. For each question, ask yourself who the reader is, what the purpose is, and which word fits exactly.
- L15: Well done. Careful word choice and the right format make people take your writing seriously. Let's begin.

### E1 — Formal email
> **From:** arjun.mehta@example.com
> **To:** ward12.office@example.gov.in
> **Date:** 3 October 2026
> **Subject:** Request to repair broken streetlights on Lake View Road, Sector 12
>
> Dear Sir/Madam,
>
> I am writing to draw your attention to the streetlights on Lake View Road, Sector 12, which have not been working for the past three weeks. As a result, the road becomes completely dark after 7 p.m. Elderly residents and students returning from tuition classes find it unsafe to walk, and two minor accidents have already been reported.
>
> I request you to look into the matter and arrange for the lights to be repaired at the earliest. The residents of our sector would be grateful for prompt action.
>
> Yours faithfully,
> Arjun Mehta
> Resident, Sector 12

### D1 — Dialogue: Report Trouble
**Kiara:** We have to hand in the report on the cleanliness drive by Friday, and I haven't even started.
**Dev:** Relax. Let's break it down. You write the introduction; I'll put together the photos.
**Kiara:** Fine, but Mrs. Rao said a report shouldn't sound like a diary. No "I felt so happy" stuff.
**Dev:** Right. It needs a heading, the writer's name, the date and place, what happened, and the outcome. Formal and to the point.
**Kiara:** I keep mixing up "affect" and "effect," too. Did the drive have a good effect, or did it affect things well?
**Dev:** "Effect" is usually the noun, meaning the result. "Affect" is usually the verb, meaning to influence. The drive had a positive effect.
**Kiara:** You're a lifesaver. Oh, and Mrs. Rao wants us to mention that the municipal team turned up late.
**Dev:** Let's put it tactfully: "The municipal team joined the volunteers at 11 a.m."
**Kiara:** Ha! You really know how to beat around the bush.
**Dev:** That's not beating around the bush. That's being diplomatic. Now, let's get cracking.

- stem: |
  What is the verb form of the adjective "strong"?
- options:
  - A: strongly
  - B: strength
  - C: stronger
  - D: strengthen
- answer: D
- explanation: |
  The suffix "-en" turns some adjectives or nouns into verbs, as in strength → strengthen and wide → widen. "Strength" is the noun and "strongly" is the adverb.

### Q21

- skill: word-formation
- difficulty: easy
- stem: |
  Which word correctly means "not responsible"?
- options:
  - A: unresponsible
  - B: disresponsible
  - C: nonresponsible
  - D: irresponsible
- answer: D
- explanation: |
  Words beginning with "r" often take the prefix "ir-," as in irregular and irrelevant. Likewise "il-" goes before "l" (illegal) and "im-" before "p" (impossible).

### Q24
- id: g8-eng-ch03-b-24
- skill: idioms-phrasal-verbs
- difficulty: hard
- stem: |
  Choose the correct phrasal verb.
  "The firefighters managed to ___ the fire within an hour."
- options:
  - A: put out
  - B: put off
  - C: put up
  - D: put on
- answer: A
- explanation: |
  "Put out" means to extinguish. "Put off" means postpone, "put up" means erect or tolerate, and "put on" means wear. The particle changes the whole meaning.
