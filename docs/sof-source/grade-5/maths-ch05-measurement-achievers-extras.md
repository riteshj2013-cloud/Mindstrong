- **id**: g5-maths-ch05-ach-x-01
- **stem**: Rajvi's hall plan is shown. Every part except the store room gets square tiles that measure 50 cm along each edge. How many such tiles does the hall need?

- **alt**: A rectangular floor plan 8 m along the bottom and 6 m on the left side. A grey dashed store room fills the top-right corner; the top edge of the tiled part next to it is 5 m and the store room's left side is 2 m.
- **options**:
  - A) 84 tiles
  - B) 168 tiles
  - C) 192 tiles
  - D) 42 tiles
- **answer**: B
- **explanation**: The store room is 8 m − 5 m = 3 m wide and 2 m deep, so the tiled area is 8 × 6 − 3 × 2 = 48 − 6 = 42 m². A 50 cm tile is half a metre each way, so 2 × 2 = 4 tiles cover 1 m², and 42 × 4 = 168 tiles are needed. 84 tiles is tempting because it takes 2 tiles for each square metre, but halving the side makes a quarter of a square metre, not a half.
- **hint**: Find the tiled area of Rajvi's floor in square metres, then work out how many 50 cm tiles fit in one square metre.

### Q02
- **id**: g5-maths-ch05-ach-x-02
- **stem**: A gravel strip 2 m wide is laid along the outside of all four edges of Ridhima's school garden, a rectangle 20 m long and 12 m wide. How many square metres of gravel are needed?
- **options**:
  - A) 144 m²
  - B) 128 m²
  - C) 240 m²
  - D) 384 m²
- **answer**: A
- **explanation**: With the gravel, the outer rectangle is 20 + 2 + 2 = 24 m long and 12 + 2 + 2 = 16 m wide, so it covers 24 × 16 = 384 m². The garden covers 20 × 12 = 240 m². The gravel covers 384 − 240 = 144 m². 128 m² is tempting because it is the garden's perimeter, 64 m, times the 2 m width, but that leaves out the four 2 m by 2 m corner squares of the strip.
- **hint**: Find the length and width of the big rectangle that holds Ridhima's garden and the gravel together, then take away the garden.
- **skill**: area_of_path_round_a_rectangle
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers

### Q03
- **id**: g5-maths-ch05-ach-x-03
- **stem**: Rishit's rectangular photo frame has an area of 96 cm² and a length of 12 cm. He bends a wire as long as the frame's perimeter into a square. What is the area of the square?
- **options**:
  - A) 25 cm²
  - B) 96 cm²
  - C) 64 cm²
  - D) 100 cm²
- **answer**: D
- **explanation**: The width is 96 ÷ 12 = 8 cm, so the perimeter is 2 × (12 + 8) = 40 cm. The square has side 40 ÷ 4 = 10 cm and area 10 × 10 = 100 cm². 25 cm² is tempting because it uses 12 + 8 = 20 cm as the perimeter, but that is only half the way round the frame.
- **hint**: Use the area of Rishit's frame to find its width first, then its perimeter, then one side of the square.
- **skill**: square_with_same_perimeter_as_rectangle
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers

### Q05
- **id**: g5-maths-ch05-ach-x-05
- **stem**: Saanvi's grandfather feeds 350 g of grain to the pigeons every morning from a 2.5 kg bag. After how many mornings will less than 500 g be left in the bag for the first time?
- **options**:
  - A) 7 mornings
  - B) 6 mornings
  - C) 5 mornings
  - D) 4 mornings
- **answer**: B
- **explanation**: 2.5 kg = 2,500 g. After 5 mornings 2,500 − 1,750 = 750 g is left, which is not less than 500 g. After 6 mornings 2,500 − 2,100 = 400 g is left, which is less than 500 g. So the answer is 6 mornings. 7 mornings is tempting because 7 × 350 g = 2,450 g nearly empties the bag, but the question asks when less than 500 g is left, not when the bag runs out.
- **hint**: Change Saanvi's grandfather's bag into grams, then take away one morning's grain at a time and check what is left.
- **skill**: repeated_subtraction_in_grams
- **difficulty**: hard
- **type**: mcq
- **pictorial**: no
- **olympiad_section**: achievers
